# History of the 13 positions and tutorial

Source review: 2026-10-05. App copy and this document share `relation-history.ts`; regenerate with `node docs/history-13/generate.mjs` (Node ≥22).

A concept’s history differs from that of its name or notation. The earliest cited attestation is not necessarily a discovery date. Lack of an established source of intent is not proof that intent was impossible. We state the evidence boundary without assigning motives to builders.

## Three evidence layers

- Mathematics: target definitions and model calculations; precision and dependencies are covered in the [audit](matematyka-13-stalych.md).
- History: mathematical texts, artefacts and research on notation establish only their stated claims. Euclid is cited through D. Joyce’s translation; modern formulas are not his literal notation.
- Interpretation: numerical proximity, scoring and the modern Golden Egg do not establish a physical function or intent. The ordinary egg and rainbow scene is illustrative; the selected L/W section has separately calculated geometry.

## All 13 positions

### 1. pi

**Concept:** In the third century BCE, Archimedes bounded circumference divided by diameter using polygons.

**Name:** ‘Pi’ names a Greek letter; the ratio was studied long before this name was adopted.

**Symbol:** Jones used π for this ratio in 1706; Euler popularized the notation in 1748.

**Mathematics / model:** For 11:7, 2B/H = 22/7 ≈ π: half the base perimeter divided by height.

**Pyramid interpretation:** Petrie discussed this relationship in 1883. This is a later interpretation of dimensions, not evidence of intentional use of π.

Sources and scope: [archimedes](#source-archimedes), [notation](#source-notation), [petrie](#source-petrie).

### 2. gamma

**Concept:** Euler studied the difference between 1+1/2+…+1/n and ln n in 1734–35; the work appeared in 1740.

**Name:** The name commemorates Euler and Mascheroni’s later calculations in 1790.

**Symbol:** Euler wrote C, Mascheroni A. γ is attested in the nineteenth century; priority for its use is disputed.

**Mathematics / model:** Target γ is the limit of Hₙ−ln n. Model ratio 2B/(H+2D) is an approximation, reciprocal to the √3 row.

**Pyramid interpretation:** The history of γ does not document its selection in the pyramid. No source of intent for this formula has been established.

Sources and scope: [notation](#source-notation).

### 3. sqrt3

**Concept:** Archimedes used bounds 265/153 < √3 < 1351/780 in Measurement of a Circle; how he obtained them is uncertain.

**Name:** ‘Square root of 3’ describes the positive number whose square is 3; it is not named after a discoverer.

**Symbol:** √ appears in Rudolff’s Coss (1525), long after Archimedes’ calculations.

**Mathematics / model:** R = (H+2D)/(2B) ≈ √3 and R = 1/Rγ. Targets √3 and γ are not exact reciprocals.

**Pyramid interpretation:** Ancient calculations of √3 do not establish use of this sum of lengths at Giza.

Sources and scope: [archimedes](#source-archimedes), [radical](#source-radical).

### 4. sqrt6

**Concept:** Book X of Euclid’s Elements studies commensurability of lengths and squares, the context of modern irrational roots.

**Name:** ‘Square root of 6’ denotes the positive number with square 6. A separate discovery date has not been established.

**Symbol:** √6 combines the radical sign, attested in Rudolff in 1525, with the number 6.

**Mathematics / model:** Target √6 = √2·√3. Also R√6 = √2·R√3, so the two rows have the same relative error.

**Pyramid interpretation:** This is an algebraically dependent result, not a second independent piece of evidence about the pyramid’s design.

Sources and scope: [euclidX](#source-euclidX), [radical](#source-radical).

### 5. sqrt2

**Concept:** Old Babylonian tablet YBC 7289 contains an accurate approximation to a square’s diagonal, today’s √2.

**Name:** ‘Square root of 2’ is an algebraic description. The tablet does not use this modern name.

**Symbol:** Modern √2 uses the radical sign in Rudolff’s tradition (1525); the tablet uses sexagesimal notation.

**Mathematics / model:** D² = B²+B² ⇒ D/B = √2 exactly, at every height of a square pyramid.

**Pyramid interpretation:** This is a property of the square base. It does not distinguish 11:7; a Babylonian tablet does not establish the Giza builders’ intent.

Sources and scope: [babylon](#source-babylon), [radical](#source-radical).

### 6. sqrt5

**Concept:** Euclid’s construction II.11 divides a segment in a ratio now expressed using √5 and the golden ratio.

**Name:** ‘Square root of 5’ describes a number with square 5: modern algebraic language for ancient geometry.

**Symbol:** √5 uses the radical sign attested in Rudolff (1525), rather than Euclid’s notation.

**Mathematics / model:** √5 = 2φ−1. Model ratio (S+B)/S = 1+2/p, p=S/A; equality with √5 requires p=φ.

**Pyramid interpretation:** This row depends on S/A. Euclid’s construction does not establish its application in the Great Pyramid.

Sources and scope: [euclidII](#source-euclidII), [radical](#source-radical).

### 7. tribonacci

**Concept:** The sequence sums three preceding terms. Feinberg described it in Fibonacci–Tribonacci in 1963.

**Name:** The article’s title documents ‘Tribonacci’, referring to Fibonacci and three summands.

**Symbol:** T is this app’s abbreviation for the constant, not evidence of a universal historical symbol.

**Mathematics / model:** Target T>1 satisfies T³=T²+T+1. Model R=(1+4√2)/(p+2), p=S/A, is an approximation.

**Pyramid interpretation:** The modern name does not date the relationship itself, but no source connects it to the builders’ intentions.

Sources and scope: [tribonacci](#source-tribonacci).

### 8. brun

**Concept:** In 1919 Brun proved convergence of the reciprocal twin-prime sum, e.g. (1/3+1/5)+(1/5+1/7)+….

**Name:** ‘Brun’s constant’ commemorates Viggo Brun and denotes that sum, rather than an edge length.

**Symbol:** Here B₂ denotes the twin-prime sum. It differs from B, the pyramid’s base side.

**Mathematics / model:** E/A = √(p²+1), p=S/A. The comparison uses estimate 1.902160583104, not a known exact value.

**Pyramid interpretation:** No theorem connecting E/A to the prime sum or source of builders’ intent has been established.

Sources and scope: [brun](#source-brun), [brunEstimate](#source-brunEstimate).

### 9. invPhi

**Concept:** In a 1597 letter to Kepler, Mästlin gave about 0.6180340 for the longer part of a unit segment divided in the golden ratio.

**Name:** ‘Inverse φ’ describes the same proportion in the opposite direction, not a new discovery.

**Symbol:** 1/φ combines division with the later symbol φ; we do not attribute ancient origins to this notation.

**Mathematics / model:** 1/φ = φ−1. Model ratio S/(S+A)=p/(p+1) is not generally equal to 1/p.

**Pyramid interpretation:** This transforms the S/A row, without providing additional independent evidence about the pyramid.

Sources and scope: [golden](#source-golden), [notation](#source-notation).

### 10. phi

**Concept:** Euclid VI, definition 3, describes extreme and mean ratio: whole to larger part as larger to smaller.

**Name:** ‘Golden section’ is attested in Martin Ohm in 1835; its originator has not been established.

**Symbol:** Cook in 1914 credits Mark Barr with proposing φ. This documents notation, not use of the ratio by Phidias.

**Mathematics / model:** φ=(1+√5)/2 and φ²=φ+1. For 11:7, S/A≈φ; the exact-φ preset selects a different H/A.

**Pyramid interpretation:** Proximity of S/A to φ is a model result. The cited sources do not document an intended golden ratio in the pyramid.

Sources and scope: [euclidVI](#source-euclidVI), [golden](#source-golden), [notation](#source-notation).

### 11. e

**Concept:** Studies of logarithms and compound interest led to e. Bernoulli studied the limit of (1+1/n)ⁿ in 1683.

**Name:** ‘Euler’s number’ commemorates the mathematician, not evidence that the concept began with him.

**Symbol:** Euler used e in a 1727–28 manuscript and in print in Mechanica (1736). Why he chose the letter is uncertain.

**Mathematics / model:** e is the natural-logarithm base. Model ratio 2θ/β≈e compares angles; degrees and radians give the same ratio.

**Pyramid interpretation:** Factor 2 is a choice of comparison formula. The history of e does not establish such a pyramid design.

Sources and scope: [e](#source-e), [notation](#source-notation).

### 12. eMinus1

**Concept:** e−1 subtracts one from e. Its historical context is the development of logarithms and the exponential function.

**Name:** ‘e minus one’ describes an operation; no separate historical name or discovery has been established.

**Symbol:** e−1 combines Euler’s symbol with subtraction. It differs from e⁻¹, which means 1/e.

**Mathematics / model:** The model subtracts one from R_e. Its absolute error equals the e row’s; its relative error differs.

**Pyramid interpretation:** This transforms the same geometric result, rather than providing another independent piece of evidence of intent.

Sources and scope: [e](#source-e), [notation](#source-notation).

### 13. eggLW

**Concept:** Christian Lange describes a modern golden-oval proposal: a plane cuts a hyperbola’s surface of revolution, with Z₀=7.65.

**Name:** ‘Golden Egg’ names the proposal and preset; its first use or ancient origin has not been established.

**Symbol:** L/W means length divided by width. Target φ is an existing constant, not a new L/W constant.

**Mathematics / model:** The zr=1 oval is not an ellipse. At 11:7 its L/W error against φ is 0.105620285%; Golden Egg chooses an angle to fit the target.

**Pyramid interpretation:** Lange documents his own interpretation. The audit does not confirm his numerical claims or claims about physical function or builders’ intent.

Sources and scope: [lange](#source-lange), [euclidVI](#source-euclidVI).

### Source criticism and limitations

MacTutor contains historical scholarship and Jeff Miller’s register referencing earlier texts; it is not an ancient document. For γ the register records conflicting attributions; we do not claim an inventor of the symbol. No separate discovery date is given for √6. Feinberg’s article attests the name and sequence in 1963, not priority for all third-order recurrences. B₂ and T are notations used here, without a claim of first historical use.

Petrie first published the book in 1883. The linked scan is a 1990 reprint of the abridged second edition of 1885. The title, preface and p. 93 (PDF page 106) were inspected: the author interprets 7:22 for height and half-perimeter, and 14:11 for the profile. Survey results are in chapter II; interpretation is in chapter IX. A later theory is not converted into a construction-era design record. The Rhind papyrus (EA10057, about 1550 BCE) is later than the Great Pyramid and is not its plan.

Lange is a primary source for his own undated interpretation. It establishes the provenance of the Golden Egg proposal, not its physical or historical speculation or global uniqueness. His 51.84° and dimensions do not agree with the audited exact model: at Z₀=7.65 the L/W=φ solution is about 51.795319256°. First use of the name has not been established. OEIS cautions about digits in the Brun estimate; a rigorous error interval remains open.

## Tutorial and interface

### 1. Start with the square

B is a base side, A its half, D the full diagonal. D/B=√2 is exact at every height. This is our starting point, not a distinguishing feature of 11:7.

`A=B/2; D=B√2`

Positions: sqrt2.

### 2. One shape parameter

H is vertical height; S is the apothem to a side midpoint. VOM is a right triangle. For 11:7, t=14/11. Scaling preserves ratios; changing t changes them.

`t=H/A; p=S/A=√(1+t²)`

Positions: phi.

### 3. Half the perimeter and π

Divide two copies of B by H. The full perimeter is 4B, so its ratio to H approximates 2π. 22/7 is not exactly π. Interpreting dimensions does not document builders’ intent.

`Rπ=2B/H=4/t; 11:7 ⇒ 22/7≈π`

Positions: pi.

### 4. The γ, √3 and √6 family

These three rows use H and D. Inverting u gives the γ row; multiplying by √2 gives the √6 row. Results are dependent; targets γ and √3 are not exact reciprocals.

`u=t/4+√2; Rγ=1/u; R√3=u; R√6=√2·u`

Positions: gamma, sqrt3, sqrt6.

### 5. The φ, 1/φ and √5 family

p determines all three results. When p=φ they match their targets exactly; at 11:7 they are approximations. Model p/(p+1) is not generally 1/p. Select a position to inspect its segments.

`p=S/A; R1/φ=p/(p+1); R√5=1+2/p`

Positions: phi, invPhi, sqrt5.

### 6. Tribonacci and Brun also depend on p

E reaches a corner; S reaches a side midpoint: E²=S²+A². Thus E/A depends on p, as does the Tribonacci sum ratio. Brun’s target is an estimate; proximity does not connect geometry to the prime sum.

`RT=(1+4√2)/(p+2); RB₂=√(p²+1)`

Positions: tribonacci, brun.

### 7. Angles, e and e−1

θ and β are complementary angles of the same triangle. Divide angle measures, not arc lengths. Subtracting one gives the e−1 row. This is one result in two forms.

`θ=atan(t); β=90°−θ; Re=2θ/β; R(e−1)=Re−1`

Positions: e, eMinus1.

### 8. The oval is a separate model

The section takes θ from the pyramid but adds a surface and Z₀. L and W are not pyramid edges. The oval is not an ellipse. Golden Egg fits its angle to φ; at 11:7 the error is 0.105620285%.

`zr=1; z=Z₀+x tan θ; Z₀=7.65; L/W≈φ`

Positions: eggLW, phi.

### 9. How to read a result and its history

R is the model result, c the target. 13 rows do not give 13 independent proofs. History separates concept, name and symbol. Calculation is distinct from hypothesis. Petrie’s later survey and the Rhind papyrus do not record builders’ intent.

`ε=|R−c|/|c|`

Positions: phi, pi, eggLW.
